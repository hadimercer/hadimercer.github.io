Comp Dash: market benchmarking on public BLS data

A data pipeline and eight-page dashboard that pulls real BLS wage data for five US metros, maps it to a fictional company’s job families and flags where pay sits outside the market range.

Status
Live
Built with
Python, PostgreSQL, Streamlit, BLS OEWS public API
Data
Real public wage data plus 800 synthetic employee records (fictional company “TechNova”)
Live demo (opens in a new tab) Code on GitHub (opens in a new tab)

The demo runs on Streamlit’s free tier and can take about 30 seconds to wake.

The HR problem

Compensation teams at mid-size companies often benchmark roles with exports from paid salary surveys and static spreadsheets. Those snapshots age quickly, do not connect to internal salary data, and give no steady view of where pay sits against the market.

Internal pay changes all year through new hires, promotions and merit increases, while market data is refreshed once a year. The distance between when market data was collected and when a pay decision is made is where risk builds, most of all in job families where the market moves fast. Comp Dash uses the Bureau of Labor Statistics wage survey (OEWS), which is free and public, as a reference where direction matters more than precision.

What I built

  • Pull current BLS wages at the 25th, 50th and 75th percentiles for 20 occupations in five metros: Austin, New York, San Francisco, Washington DC and Denver.
  • Load TechNova’s salary and grade data from CSV files that are checked against the required format before anything is saved.
  • Compare internal pay ranges with the market side by side by job family, level and city, switch between percentiles, and see each gap in dollars and percent.
  • See which roles sit below the market’s 25th percentile or above its 75th, with a chart of how large the gaps are.
  • Review gender pay gaps by job family and level, with gaps over 5% flagged for Total Rewards review.
  • Compare one role across all five cities, and check every pipeline run in a log with its status, record counts and duration.

How it works

Two write paths feed one read path. The BLS pipeline and the CSV upload write to PostgreSQL separately, the dashboard reads from both, and every write is recorded in a pipeline run log.

Pipeline architecture

Write path 1

BLS pipeline

Reads occupation codes from the crosswalk, builds data series for 5 metros, 20 occupations and 6 data types, requests them 50 at a time and saves wages by occupation, metro and year.

Requirements: FR-01 to FR-04

Write path 2

CSV upload

Checks every file against the required columns and rejects the whole file on any error. Valid files update the 800 employee records and 150 salary bands.

Requirements: FR-05 to FR-07

Dashboard

Eight Streamlit pages read from both sources, with the last BLS refresh and the last upload shown in the sidebar.

The SOC crosswalk

BLS publishes wages by Standard Occupational Classification (SOC) code, not by job title. A crosswalk maps each of TechNova’s 38 internal roles to one of 20 SOC codes, with a note on how close each match is. It is handled as a controlled document with its own change log, because every benchmark on the dashboard depends on it.

Working with a public data API

The pipeline calls the BLS public API with a registered key: more than 600 data series per run, sent in batches of 50. If a batch fails, it logs a warning, skips that batch and keeps the existing data. The connection is documented in an OpenAPI specification.

Technology decision: Power BI to Streamlit

The requirements document specifies a Power BI dashboard. I built it in Streamlit instead and recorded why: sharing a Power BI report outside an organization needs a paid license, while Streamlit Community Cloud hosts it free and public. Every dashboard requirement (FR-11 to FR-16) is met in Streamlit. In a real project, this change would go through a formal change request.

What this shows

  • Benchmarking pay the way a Total Rewards team reads it: by job family, level and city, against the 25th, 50th and 75th percentiles.
  • Pay equity framed as monitoring, not legal compliance determination: gaps are flagged for people to review.
  • Turning a public data source into something usable: a job-to-occupation crosswalk, format checks before any write, and a log of every run.
  • Judgment about tools: a change from the written spec, made on purpose and documented.
  • 18 written functional requirements, prioritized and traced back to the objectives.

Limits

Comp Dash is a personal project. The wage data is real and public; the company, its 800 employees and their salaries are synthetic.

The pay equity view flags gaps for a Total Rewards team to review. It is not a legal pay equity analysis and not a tool for setting anyone’s pay.

BLS data is published once a year and is less precise than commercial salary surveys, so it shows direction, not an exact market rate.
